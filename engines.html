PROJECT 5

A 100 lbf turbofan for small UAVs

The brief was an engine producing 100 lbf of thrust at Mach 0.8 and 35,000 ft, a size class used in drones and small unmanned aircraft. We ran trade studies on bypass ratio, compressor pressure ratio and fan pressure ratio, then sized every station of the engine.

We settled on a compressor pressure ratio of 12, a bypass ratio of 2.5 and a fan pressure ratio of 1.5. A higher bypass ratio would have improved efficiency, but the drop in specific thrust meant much larger flow areas for the same thrust. An engine this small has no room for active blade cooling, so we set the turbine inlet temperature at 1,600 K, safely below the roughly 1,760 K limit of silicon carbide ceramic-matrix turbine blades.

My role: wrote the Python code for the cycle analysis and the parameter sweeps behind the trade studies.

PROJECT 6

An axial compressor stage running on helium

We designed a single axial compressor stage for helium: 20 kg/s at a pressure ratio of 1.7, spinning at 23,000 RPM. Because blade speed grows with radius, we analyzed the stage at three radial slices from hub to tip, building the velocity triangles and Euler work at each one.

Two constraints drove the design: the relative inlet Mach number had to stay below 1.2 to avoid strong shocks, and the flow turning across each blade had to stay under 20° to avoid separation. The tip, where blade speed is highest, came closest to the Mach limit. We also checked an off-design case: cutting mass flow by 5% raised the pressure ratio to 1.76.

My role: wrote the analysis code, produced the graphs, and analyzed the tip slice.
PROJECT 4

A 40 MW turboshaft for power and marine use

We designed a 40 MW turboshaft, the kind of engine used for power generation and ship propulsion, first as an ideal cycle and then with realistic component losses. Choosing compressor, burner, turbine and mechanical efficiencies of 0.90, 0.96, 0.90 and 0.99 cut thermal efficiency roughly in half. The real design uses a lower pressure ratio (18 instead of 24.75) and needs about a third more airflow to deliver the same power.

My role: built the component-efficiency sensitivity plots, for example sweeping turbine efficiency from 0.85 to 0.92 to see how much each loss mattered, and ran the sanity check against textbook equations.
PROJECT 3

Reverse-engineering the Pratt & Whitney JT9D

The JT9D, which powered the Boeing 747, was one of the first high-bypass turbofans. We reconstructed its cycle: bypass ratio, pressure ratios through the low- and high-pressure compressors, fuel-to-air ratio, nozzle exit conditions, thrust, fuel consumption and efficiency. The fan alone produced about 34,300 lbf of the engine's 43,500 lbf rating, showing how much of a high-bypass engine's thrust comes from the fan.
